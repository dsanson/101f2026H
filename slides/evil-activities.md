# Review

## The Argument from Pointless Suffering

::::: {#argument-AS .argument}
- (PS1) There is **pointless** suffering in the world
- (PS2) If there is **pointless** suffering in the world, then there is no
  **omnibeing**.
- (PS3) So, there is no **omnibeing**.
:::::

-  Four objections to PS1: Appreciated Goods, Building Character, Free Will, Hidden Reasons.


## Appreciated Goods

-  PS1 is false. The point of suffering is that it allows us to appreciate good things.
-  First objection: someone who experiences less pleasure and then a lot more pleasure can appreciate how much better the greater pleasure is, without ever suffering.
-  Second objection: We endure more suffering than is needed to appreciate good things.

:::notes
-   overcast day vs. midwestern winter
-   sore back vs. chronic back pain
:::

## Building Character

-  PS1 is false. The point of suffering is to build character (courage, empathy, loyalty, perseverance, self-control, forgiveness, trustworthiness, ...)
-  Objection: Not all suffering builds character.

::: notes
- without suffering, there is no danger. Without facing danger, cannot develop
  courage.
- Without profound acts of betrayal, there could be no profound acts of
  forgiveness.
- Without the horrors and hardships of war, no one could reach the levels of
  courage and selflessness that soldiers attain.
- Since these sufferings are necessary in order to have the best kind of
  world---one with courageous and forgiving people---we can see why God would
  allow them.

But:

- soliders who develop PTSD instead of courage.
- torture followed by death without chance to build character.
:::

## Free Will

-  PS1 is false. Suffering is an inevitable consequence of free will, and the value free will outweighs the badness of suffering.
-  First objection: Not all suffering: disease, natural disaster, wolves.
-  Second objection: Why couldn't God intervene to block the worst consequences of free will? (Toddler, chainsaw)
-  Third objection: If God is omniscient, we don't have free will anyway.

## Hidden Reasons

-  PS1 is false. All suffering serves a greater purpose, but often that purpose is hidden to us.
-  Objection: This is *possible*---we cannot rule it out with 100% certainty. But it is wildly implausible, and something you should not believe. (Nornia analogy)

## An All-But-Omnibeing?

-  Maybe there is no omnibeing, no being that is all-knowing, all-powerful, and all-good/morally perfect.
-  There are other reasons to worry about the possibility of an omnibeing: nobody can have the power to bring about someone else's free choice; nobody can know beforehand what that free choice will be; even the best possible being would have to make moral compromises, e.g., between justice and forgiveness.
-  What if God is not an omnibeing, but an all-but-omnibeing? Someone who is *very very* powerful, knows *everything that can be known*, and is *very very* good?

## The Argument from Pointless Suffering Against an All-But-Omnibeing

::: {#argument-AS .argument}
- (PV1) There is **pointless** suffering in the world that an all-but-omnibeing could have prevented.
- (PV2) If there is **pointless** suffering in the world of this sort, then
  there is no all-but-omnibeing.
- (PV3) So, there is no all-but-omnibeing.
:::

PV1 is harder to defend than PS1. But you might agree that, upon reflection, surely an all-but-omnibeing could have prevented at least some of the pointless suffering we find in the world, was in a position to know about it, and would have cared to do so.


# Your Thoughts 

## Overview of Writing Exercise (approx 35-40 minutes)

-   Spend a moment gathering your thoughts.
-   Spend three minutes sharing your thoughts with someone else.
-   Spend ten minutes writing up your thoughts in a paragraph.
-   Share what you have written and get/give constructive feedback.
-   Revise.
-   Share what you have written to get/give critical feedback.

## Gather Your Thoughts

:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="80%"}
What do you see as the strongest possible objection to the Argument from Pointless
Suffering? Which premise should someone who believes in an omnibeing (or an
all-but-omnibeing) deny, and why? Is your objection the same as one of the four discussed? If so, how do you respond to the
objection(s) from the book? If not, what objections do you anticipate?

(You can do this in your head, or you can jot down notes for yourself.)
:::

::: {.column width="20%"}
:::{.timer #initial-thoughts seconds=120 starton=interaction}
:::
:::

::::

## Share Your Thoughts

:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="80%"}
Turn to someone sitting next to you, compare notes, and discuss. (If needed, three of you should form a group of three.)
Don't let one person dominate. Be sure that each person gets a chance to share their thoughts.
:::

::: {.column width="20%"}
:::{.timer #share-thoughts seconds=180 starton=interaction}
:::
:::

::::

## Put Your Thoughts in Writing

:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="80%"}
Write a short argumentative paragraph, in which you state your objection as clearly as possible, 
present the strongest anticipated response to your objection, and explain why you think that response does not work.

If you have time, revise your paragraph for clarity and concision: omit needless words; try to say exactly what you mean; explain everything as explicitly as possible.
:::

::: {.column width="20%"}
:::{.timer #writing seconds=600 starton=interaction}
:::
:::

::::

## Share Your Writing

:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="80%"}
Turn to *somebody else* sitting near you, and exchange essays. (If our number is odd, three of you need to do a three-way share: A shares with B, B with C, and C with A.)

As you read your peer's writing, focus first on understanding and constructive ideas. If there is something you don't understand, or find unclear, mark it as unclear. If you have an idea about how they could develop their point further, jot it down. Avoid, for the moment, raising objections.
:::

::: {.column width="20%"}
:::{.timer #reading-1 seconds=180 starton=interaction}
:::
:::

::::

## Receive Feedback

:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="80%"}
Pass the papers back to their authors. Pick one author to "go first". Share your feedback with the author and discuss for one minute. Then switch roles for the second minute. If you are in a three-way share, switch roles after 45 seconds, and  then again at 1 minute and 30 seconds.
:::

::: {.column width="20%"}
:::{.timer #constructive-feedback seconds=140 starton=interaction}
:::
:::

::::

## Revise

:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="80%"}
Revise your writing in light of the feedback you just received.
:::

::: {.column width="20%"}
:::{.timer #revising seconds=300 starton=interaction}
:::
:::

::::

## Share Your Revisions

:::: {.columns}

::: {.column width="80%"}
Turn to *somebody else* sitting near you, and exchange essays. (If needed, some other group of three should do a three-way share this time.)

This time, I want you to focus on objections. Think about what your peer is arguing, and try to come up with at least two possible objections. Think about which of those objections you think is the strongest.
:::

::: {.column width="20%"}
:::{.timer #critical-feedback seconds=180 starton=interaction}
:::
:::

::::

## Share Objections

:::: {.columns}


::: {.column width="80%"}
Pass the papers back to their authors. Pick one author to "go first". Share your objections with the author. Discuss. After two minutes, switch roles. If you are in a three-way share, switch roles after 1 minute and 30 seconds, and again after 3 minutes.
:::

::: {.column width="20%"}
:::{.timer #discuss-objections seconds=270 starton=interaction}
:::
:::
