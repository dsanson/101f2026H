# Meaning, Time, and Size

## {.center}

> It is often remarked that nothing we do now will matter in a million years.
> But if that is true, then by the same token, nothing that will be the case in
> a million years matters now. In particular, it does not matter now that in a
> million years nothing we do now will matter. Moreover, even if what we did now
> were going to matter in a million years, how could that keep our present
> concerns from being absurd? If their mattering now is not enough to accomplish
> that, how would it help if they mattered a million years from now?
> [@nagel1971, p. 716]

---

:::argument
- (MY1) Nothing you do now will matter in a million years.
- (MY2) If nothing you do now will matter in a million years, then nothing you do now matters.
- (MY3) So, nothing you do now matters.
:::

:::notes
Why accept (MY2)? Is there something especially important about what matters in a million years, as opposed to what matters now?
:::

---

:::argument
- (YM1) Nothing that will be the case in a million years matters now.
- (YM2) If nothing that will be the case in a million years matters now, then nothing that will be the case in a million years matters.
- (YM3) So, nothing that will be the case in a million years matters.
- (YM4) So, *it does not matter* that nothing you do now will matter in a million years.
:::

---

:::argument
- (TS1) You are a tiny speck in a vast universe.
- (TS2) So, your life is meaningless.
:::

:::notes
What is the implicit premise?
:::

---

:::argument
- (CT1) Your life lasts a mere instant on the cosmic time scale.
- (CT2) So, your life is meaningless.
:::

:::notes
What is the implicit premise?
:::

---

If our lives are meaningless given our present size and duration, wouldn't they be equally meaningless if we were huge or immortal?

---

> Because we are going to die, all chains of justification must leave off in
> mid-air: one studies and works to earn money to pay for clothing, housing,
> entertainment, food, to sustain oneself from year to year, perhaps to support
> a family and pursue a career---but to what final end? All of it is an
> elaborate journey leading nowhere.

---

:::argument
- (FE1) Every end you achieve is meaningful only as an instrument to achieving some further end. 
- (FE2) When you die, the last end you achieved cannot serve as an instrument to achieving any further ends.
- (FE3) So, if you are mortal, then none of the ends you achieve are meaningful.
:::

:::notes
Objections?
:::

---

Instrumental value
:   Something has instrumental value when it is valuable for the sake of something else.

Intrinsic value
:   Something has intrinsic value when it is valuable for its own sake, not (just) for the sake of something else.

:::notes

Are there things in your (short, small) life that have intrinsic value?

pleasure, friends, relationships, ... oneself?
:::

# Immortality and Boredom

## {.center}

:::{.example title=""}
The Court physician to a sixteenth-century Emperor creates an elixir of life: anyone who drinks it regularly will live forever, without any outward aging.
His daughter Elina, at the time 42 years old, drinks it. 300 years later, she finds that her
unending life has come to a state of boredom, indifference and coldness. Everything is joyless: 'In the end it is the same', she says, 'Singing and silence'. She refuses to take the elixir again, and she dies. [@williams1973b, 82]
:::

:::notes

Why did Elina become bored? Are there things she could have done to remain engaged?

:::

---

> Her problem lay in having been at it for too long. Her trouble was it seems, boredom: a boredom connected with the fact that everything that could happen and make sense to one particular human being of 42 had already happened to her. Or, rather, all the sorts of things that could make sense to one woman of a certain character; for Elina has a certain character, and indeed, except for her accumulating memories of earlier times, and no doubt some changes of style to suit the passing centuries, seems always to have been much the same sort of person. [@williams1973b, 90]

---

:::argument
- (IB1) Either Elina remains a woman of a certain character with certain interests, ends, and values, or she radically changes that character, interests, ends, and values.
- (IB2) If she stays the same, she will become bored enough to wish herself dead.
- (IB3) If she radically changes, she will become a completely different person, and what is of value to that person is of no value to the person she is now.
- (IB4) Either way, immortality is of no value to the person she is now.
:::

## {.center .smaller}

::: {.example title=""}
Bowerick Wowbagger the Infinitely Prolonged was a man with a purpose. Not a very
good purpose, as he would have been the first to admit, but it was at least a
purpose, and it did at least keep him on the move. He had had his immortality
inadvertently thrust upon him by an unfortunate accident with an irrational
particle accelerator, a liquid lunch and a pair of rubber bands. To begin with
it was fun; he had a ball, living dangerously, taking risks, cleaning up on
high-yield long-term investments, and just generally outliving the hell out of
everybody. But things began to pall. The merry smiles he used to wear at
other people's funerals began to fade. He began to despise the Universe in
general, and everybody in it in particular.

This was the point at which he conceived his purpose, the thing that would drive
him on, and which, as far as he could see, would drive him on forever: He would
insult the Universe. That is, he would insult everybody in it. Individually,
personally, one by one, and (this was the thing he really decided to grit his
teeth over) in alphabetical order. [@adams1982]
:::

## Reflection and Discussion Questions

1.  If given the chance, would you choose to live an immortal life? How would you go about it, to avoid being bored?
2.  What is the relationship between boredom, having goals and projects that you value and keep your interest, and being curious?
3.  Can having a deadline make a project more meaningful? Is a short life more meaningful than an immortal one?
4.  You are just a short-lived tiny speck in a vast and long-lasting universe. Why does it *feel* like being bigger would make you more meaningful?
